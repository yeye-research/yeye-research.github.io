Background

I work independently on evaluation methodology for text-overlap and citation-verification tools. The starting point was practical rather than academic: in my own document workflow, a checker would report a citation as found on the strength of ten or twelve characters shared with a source that never contained the claim.

After the same failure had turned up often enough, checking by hand stopped being enough. The question became measurable: if a checker says a sentence is sourced, how often is it wrong, and what does that rate depend on? Answering it needed three things I did not have — a corpus I controlled, a ground truth I was willing to defend in public, and a statistic that would survive someone trying to break it. Building those three is what this work is.

Concretely, I assembled a corpus of 46 publicly available technical articles and transcripts (about 1.28 million characters), cut 308 sentences out of it for verification, and constructed 1,803 matched negative pairs against them. The human side is 100 blind labels collected from three raters across five rating passes, including two independent raters working from the same written criteria. The software side is a released set of scripts plus an independent MATLAB re-computation, so that every number can be re-derived from the data rather than taken on trust.

The parts with no off-the-shelf answer were the ones worth the time: a stratified sampling design so that a hundred labels would be informative rather than merely numerous; an annotation protocol whose criteria are written down before anyone sees the items; and a significance treatment built on simultaneous bands, because a twelve-point threshold sweep invites exactly the comparison that pointwise intervals cannot support.

I work alone and without funding. That has one advantage worth stating plainly — nothing in the analysis is shaped by a sponsor's expectation — and one cost, which I try to pay openly: where a number rests on a judgement call, the call is written down next to the number instead of being left implicit, and where a result came out negative, it is reported as the result.

How I work

  • Corpus first, claims second. Every statement I make is tied to a countable item in a dataset I can hand over.
  • Written criteria before labels. Raters see the rules before they see the items — otherwise the labels measure the rater, not the material.
  • Negative results are results. A metric that fails is a finding to be reported, not a setback to be smoothed over.
  • Recompute, don't trust. An independent implementation in a second language is the minimum bar for a number I am willing to publish.

Research interests

I study how automated systems decide whether a sentence is supported by a source, and what those decisions silently commit their operators to.

  • Citation verification and attribution evaluation
  • Threshold audits and cost-sensitive decision making
  • Non-identifiability of lexical-overlap metrics
  • Reliability of human annotation in evaluation
  • Reproducibility of empirical claims

Current work

What a Lexical Threshold Commits You To: Auditing Set-Overlap Metrics in Citation Verification — working paper, manuscript in preparation.

Its central result is negative: substituting a set-overlap index for a threshold sweep does not work. The paper then reports the two consequences that this failure forces, and states plainly where its own evidence stops.
